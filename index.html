1. Problem of interest

1.1 Context

The thyroid gland is a butterfly-shaped endocrine gland that releases hormones to control the body’s metabolism, energy and growth. The removal of the thyroid gland (thyroidectomy) is a surgical intervention offered to patients with thyroid cancers, goiters, Graves disease and thyroid nodules, with thyroid cancer accounting for the bulk of cases due to its high prevalence. Thyroid cancer is the second most common cancer in females between 25 and 49 years old (NCCS, 2026), and almost 95% of cases require surgery as an effective treatment option (Gomez-Saiz, 2013). The recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) (Figure 1) is an important nerve that controls voice, breathing and swallowing functions through activation of the vocalis muscle, and is a key nerve at risk during thyroidectomies due its close positioning posterior to the thyroid with an injury rate of about 8.2% in Singapore (Soong et al., 2026). The presence of large tumours in thyroid cancer can obstruct the surgeon’s field view of the RLN, increasing the risk of injury.

RLN injuries can be temporary or permanent, with a permanent injury rate of 1%, and can either be unilateral or bilateral. While unilateral RLN injury can cause swallowing difficulties and voice changes (Culp & Patel, 2023), bilateral injury can cause serious complications like respiratory disease (Culp & Patel, 2023). RLN injury can be classified into two subtypes: type I (segmental injury) or type II (global injury). Type I injuries account for 70% of cases, and are often caused by clamping, traction, compression or thermal injury and have the potential to be corrected intraoperatively if a clip, suture, vessel or fibrotic band entrapping the nerve at this point is removed on time (Wu et al., 2014).

Intra-operative nerve monitoring (IONM) system is during thyroidectomies to guide surgical decision making during thyroidectomies by allowing for RLN identification and determination of functional intactness, for surgeons to preserve the RLN or to change their operative course if they determine the nerve to be damaged. To date, there is only one IONM system that is widely distributed worldwide — Medtronic’s Neural Integrity Monitor (NIM), with the most recent model being the NIM Vital. Since the RLN controls the vocalis muscle, IONM determines RLN functional intactness by determining the RLN’s ability to stimulate the vocalis muscle. This is achieved by 1) electrical stimulation of the RLN and 2) measurement of the action potential fired by the vocalis muscle as a result of RLN stimulation. 1.2 Overview of problem

A key issue in i-IONM is its retrospective nature of nerve monitoring, as it requires the surgeon to manually switch between their dissection instruments and the stimulator probe to check for RLN function. These checking points are highly dependent on the surgeon’s choice of timepoints and frequency to probe for RLN function, and nerve injury can easily be missed between these checking points. In Singapore, the use of i-IONM shows no statistical difference in the RLN injury rate (Leow et al., 2020). Considering how RLN injury can be prevented with timely intervention (Wu et al., 2014), i-IONM’s inability to allow for early detection of RLN compromise for prospective prevention of nerve injury is a huge concern.

A seemingly clear way to address the retrospective nature of i-IONM exists: Continuous-IONM (c-IONM) is a variation of IONM that involves stimulation of RLN through an Automatic Periodic Stimulation (APS) clip electrode that is attached to the vagus nerve, the main branch which the RLN is connected to. Since this electrode is securely positioned for RLN stimulation throughout the surgery, it is able to automatically stimulate the RLN and provide feedback on the EMG activity of the vocalis muscle every 2 seconds. However, the adoption of this method has been sparse: a study found that although 90.3% of surgeons were familiar with c-IONM, only 9.7% chose to use it (Poveda et al., 2024) even despite the fact that c-IONM has been shown to improve surgical outcomes. c-IONM halved the occurrence of vocal cord paralysis to 4.9% as compared to i-IONM and showed a 30-fold decrease in permanent injury. When asked why they do not use c-IONM, surgeons often quote reasons such as the extra step of dissecting the carotid sheath that cause additional surgical risk and inconvenience to the procedure.

Aside from the lack of prospective monitoring of nerve intactness, i-IONM also has a low positive predictive value of 63% (Schneider et al., 2017). A common cause of false negative signals (Figure 3) is ETT displacement (Wu et al., 2014) resulting in improper contact between the vocalis muscles and recording electrodes. False loss of signal creates time wasted in constant checking and troubleshooting to get the correct signal. This often involves re-positioning of the ETT, which can only be done by estimation as the contact point of the electrodes is not visible to the surgeon. This troubleshooting protocol is executed in up to 23% of surgeries, adding 5-10 minutes to total surgery time (Ansari et al., 2024). A survey we conducted with 30 surgeons similarly showed ETT displacement to be among the top 3 technical challenges faced during IONM operation.

We also witnessed the unreliability of signals in our clinical immersions, where high levels of off-field feedback created noisy surgical environments (Figure 4), prompting surgeons to toggle monitoring on and off and further contributing to the workflow burden in manual stimulation of the RLN. This issue is seen in both i-IONM and c-IONM methods which both rely on the signal from the ETT recording electrodes as a measure of nerve function.
